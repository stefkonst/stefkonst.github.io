Five ways to print a small thing

At CSEM, David transfers advanced polymer microfabrication to industry, working across CAD, materials, optics and bioengineering. Part quality is a large share of the job: surface roughness (Ra) on printed parts came down by more than 8×.

01 · polymer · life sciences, watchmaking

Micro-SLA

High-resolution resin printing, taken from process development to industrial transfer. Complex microstructures with fine features and a surface finish good enough for both functional and aesthetic parts.

02 · polymer mold → solid metal

3D-LIGA

Traditional LIGA makes 2D molds. Here the polymer molds are printed in full 3D, directly on 4-inch gold-coated wafers, then filled by electroplating. The result is fully metallic, watch-scale parts with low roughness.

Schematic of the process, not to scale.

03 · micromechanics, RF

E-LAM

Lithography-based electroforming: print a high-resolution polymer template, deposit metal over it, and optionally remove the polymer. What stays can be a metal part thin enough to be a shell, with a very clean surface.

Follow the gear

04 · ceramic

Alumina you can lose on your desk

Ceramic stereolithography on the BMF S240. High-resolution ceramic microfabrication stays hard because of material limits and the difficulty of debinding and sintering. This route gives dense, high-precision parts.

05 · metal

Metal from a resin printer

Lithography-based metal manufacturing in 316L stainless and Ti64 titanium, on the Incus Hammer Lab35. The work is in the steps after printing: cleaning, debinding and sintering. Titanium needs especially tight control during heat treatment. The workflows hold dimensions, minimise defects and reach high density.

06 · master's thesis

A pressure sensor that disappears

Neurosurgery sees 13.8 million cases a year, and brain swelling can turn severe fast. David designed, built and tested a wireless intracranial pressure sensor made only of bioresorbable materials, using additive manufacturing. It could be implanted temporarily and would not need to be taken out. He compared laminated PGS, laminated PoMaC and cast PoMaC dielectrics, and measured capacitance against force and hysteresis.

08 · published · PET imaging

Getting the light out

PET scanners detect gamma rays through flashes of light in crystals such as LYSO and BGO. The high refractive index traps much of that light inside. The study tried Bragg reflectors, metal coatings and photonic crystal structures to get it out. David characterised the crystals, analysed the signals in MATLAB, and designed and built the coincidence setup used for the measurements. Read the paper ↗

09 · wearable

Happy Feet

A sock for people with flat feet, worn inside the shoe. An array of PDMS capacitive sensors reads the pressure under the foot, and inflatable cushions counteract the collapse of the arch. The air needs no pump: a chamber at the heel is compressed by the wearer's own weight at each step, and valves decide where the air goes.

10 · cleanroom

Beams that bend when heated

SiO₂ cantilevers with a chromium track. Joule heating makes the two layers expand differently, so the beam bends. David simulated the KOH etch to guarantee full release, ran the lithography and wet etch, and characterised them with four-point resistance measurements and resonance sweeps.

11 · electronics

A remote that ignores the sun

Press a button, toggle a relay, in infrared. It has to ignore daylight and lamps, and several identical units must share a room, so each press sends a signature: bursts of seven 1 ms pulses at 10 % duty and 1 A peak, in 10 ms frames. Two AAA batteries run it, and it sleeps about 99 % of the time.

12 · mechanism design

Pointing a telescope mirror without friction

A dynamically balanced two-axis mechanism for the secondary mirror of the SOFIA telescope. Anything that slides or rolls sheds particles onto the optics, so every joint is a flexure. After comparing concepts, the team refined an "Improved Cardan" design for its compact compensation, constant rotational inertia and simplicity.
