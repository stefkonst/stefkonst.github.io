Active membrane wings / Analysis

Analysis and discussion

What the high-speed videos of the robot say, read as closely as they allow. The main question: does powering the wing change how the robot flaps? At 8 Hz and 1200 V, the run with the wing powered swings 20 % further than the run with only the core powered, and its cycle has a different shape. Everything on this page comes from the video.

The 20 %

With the wing powered, the robot strokes 20 % further and its cycle loses most of its lopsidedness. Both runs reach the same lowest point; the extra stroke is all on one side, where a tense edge would hold the wing back. A drift of the natural frequency could change the size but not the shape, so the wing's state changed the robot's mechanics.

  • Measured (d): 24.3 ± 1.0° peak to peak with the wing powered, 20.2 ± 0.7° with it unpowered, 35 cycles each; the ranges barely touch (21.7–25.1° against 19.7–22.4°). In the fundamental: 12.4° against 9.1°, +37 %. The gap is four times the cycle-to-cycle spread.
  • Shape (e): both cycles reach the same lowest point. With the wing powered, the wing waits longer at the bottom, then swings further on the other side. Unpowered, the cycle is more triangular and peaks earlier. The gap is on one side of the stroke only.
  • b, c: twelve phases of one cycle, in mm from the hinge. In both runs the leading edge bends as it turns; how much cannot be compared between the two, because the side views were filmed from different heights.

Drift or wing?

The two runs were filmed 20 minutes apart (12:22 and 12:42), and the camera was moved and zoomed in between. The 8 Hz drive sits on the steep side of the robot's resonance, where a 1 % shift in natural frequency changes the stroke by about 5 %. So a drift of the robot between the runs could make a gap of this size on its own. The video gives two tests.

  • So: the size of the fundamental alone could be a drift. The shape could not. Unpowered, the stroke carries 2.4× more 2nd harmonic even though it is smaller; with the same lopsided spring, a larger stroke would carry about $(12.4/9.1)^2 = 1.9\times$ more, not less. Powering the wing changed the robot's restoring force, not just its natural frequency.
  • What is not settled: how much of the 20 % comes from that change and how much from drift. Both are in the data.

Shape of the stroke

  • Equation: a restoring moment with a quadratic part, $M = k\theta + \beta\theta^2$, turns a pure 8 Hz drive into an 8 Hz stroke plus a 16 Hz part of size $\approx \beta A_1^2 / (2k)$ times the response at 16 Hz. The 2nd harmonic measures the asymmetry $\beta$ of the spring.
  • Measured: $A_2/A_1^2$ = 0.0046 /° powered and 0.021 /° unpowered. The spring is about 4.5× more lopsided with the wing unpowered.
  • Ripple: the unpowered stroke also carries a 56 Hz ripple, the 7th harmonic, locked to the stroke; the powered one does not. A tense membrane has modes that a slack one does not.
  • Validate: the same robot, same camera, wing on and off every 10 s: the 2nd harmonic should jump with the wing's state, block by block.

Natural frequency

The robot behaves as one resonator: the stroke peaks at 8 Hz, next to its natural frequency of 8.4 Hz, with a damping ratio of 0.07. The peak is half the height that damping allows because the DEA force lags the voltage by about 40 ms, the same order as on the bench. The resonance sits well below the 20–25 Hz the design assumed.

  • Equation (c): $\theta_1(h) = K\,[(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2]^{-1/2}\,[1+(2\pi f\tau)^2]^{-1/2}$, $r = f/f_n$. With $\zeta$ 0.07 from the decays: $f_n$ 8.38 ± 0.20 Hz, force lag $\tau$ 38 ± 13 ms. Without the lag the peak would be twice as high.
  • Trend (d): in the free decays the frequency rises as the motion dies out, about 8.0 Hz at 3.4° to 9 Hz near 1°: the spring softens at large stroke. This is also why the measured peak in a is broader than the model's.
  • Trend (e): at 1 Hz the stroke goes as $V^2$; at 8 Hz, 1400 V adds only 8 % over 1200 V.
  • f: the stroke at 8 Hz against $f_n$, all else fixed: 8.95 Hz would give the unpowered fundamental. Read with the shape test above: a shift like this may be part of the gap, not all of it.

A model that explains it

A second mode, the wing bending with its tense edge, explains both changes: at 0 V it sits at 17 Hz, on the stroke's second harmonic, where it loads the stroke and amplifies its 16 Hz part. The bench wing model places a 17 Hz mode at about 4 mN of edge tension, which the roll loses at 1.2 kV, so voltage slackens the edge and moves the mode out of the band. One chain links bench and robot: voltage removes tension, tension sets the mode, the mode limits the stroke.

  • Stroke: $\ddot\theta + 2\zeta\omega\dot\theta + \omega^2\theta + \beta\theta^2 = G u(t) - \mu\omega_w^2 q$, with the core's force $u$ lagged ($\tau\dot u + u = \sin^4\pi f t$).
  • Spring asymmetry (b): the ratio $X_2/X_1^2$ does not depend on when the clock starts, so it gives $\beta$ for each run without a drive signal. Powered: one real value at 7 and 8 Hz and at 1.4 kV, and with it the model reproduces the powered 2nd harmonic (0.76° against 0.72°). Unpowered: opposite sign, three times larger, and not real: a phase lag at 16 Hz, so a resonance near twice the stroke frequency. A drift of $f_n$ changes these values by less than 10 %.
  • Wing mode (c, d): $\ddot q + 2\zeta_w\omega_w\dot q + \omega_w^2 q = -\ddot\theta$, the leading edge bending with the tense membrane; the video sees $\theta + q$. Fitted to five clock-free numbers of the unpowered run: $f_w$ 17.0 ± 1.1 Hz, damping 0.05, coupling 0.21. It gives the size (9.34° against 9.07°) and the 2nd harmonic (1.85° against 1.71°) within 1 s.d., and the cycle shape; it misses the 3rd harmonic. A static one-sided spring gets the size but not the shape.
  • Edge tension (e): the bench wing model's edge mode is 17 Hz at $T \approx$ 4.2 mN, against 50–96 mN on the bench mount, and 10 Hz when slack. At 1.2 kV the roll loses 3–38 mN, so it goes slack and the mode leaves the band.
  • So: at 0 V the barely tense edge gives the wing a mode at 17 Hz that takes energy from the stroke and amplifies its 16 Hz part; voltage slackens the edge, removes the mode and frees the stroke. Prediction to check: the edge on the robot carries about 4 mN at rest.

Further analysis

The most interesting result in these videos is that the robot flaps differently when its wing is powered: 20 % more stroke, and a cycle whose shape no drift of the natural frequency can produce. A model with one wing mode explains both: at 0 V the barely tense edge (about 4 mN) gives the wing a mode at 17 Hz that loads the stroke and amplifies its 16 Hz part; voltage slackens the edge and removes it. Two things limit the claim: the camera moved and 20 minutes passed between the two runs, so part of the gap may be drift, and the leading-edge bending cannot be compared across the two side views because they were filmed from different heights (the diagonal pair bends the same way in both states). One session with the wing switched on and off every 10 s, at 8 Hz and then across 6–11 Hz, under a fixed camera, will separate the two: a wing effect follows the switching, a drift follows the clock. If it holds, the wing becomes a way to trim the robot's stroke amplitude.

Method

Phantom VEO 710L, 200 fps, 832 × 600 px; 0.152 mm/px (0.135 for the unpowered side view) from the ruler. Per run: background = 97th percentile of each pixel; leading edge = upper edge of the region darker than the background by more than 35 grey levels, in columns across the wing, fitted with a line (stroke angle) and a parabola (bend). Peak to peak per cycle; harmonics by least-squares Fourier fit at the refined drive frequency, spread from 0.5 s blocks. Decays: damped cosine and the frequency between successive extremes. Switch-on transient: linear oscillator from rest driven by $\sin^4(\pi f t)$ (sine-squared voltage, force ∝ $V^2$). Code: flapping/code (track.py, pair.py, decay.py, ring1hz.py, sweepfit.py, onset_fit.py, le_bend.py); figures: results/figures_video.py, results/figures_tests.py.
